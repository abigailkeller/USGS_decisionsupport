    How is this optimization tailored to my EGC population?

    1. The optimization simulates recruits entering the population based on the timing of when recruits enter your population (i.e., proportion of recruits settling in April vs. June).
    2. The optimization finds the best trap schedule for next year based on the estimated size-structured abundance in the last year of your data.
    3. The optimization simulates variation in recruitment based on the estimated mean recruit abundance and level of variation across years, if multiple consecutive years are uploaded.
    4. The optimization uses your model’s estimated shape of overwinter survival (overall magnitude and strength of size-dependence), if multiple consecutive years are uploaded.

    How is uncertainty accounted for in this optimization?

    This optimization accounts for both parameter uncertainty and natural variability. To account for parameter uncertainty, the optimization uses your model’s posterior samples that quantify uncertainty in parameters like mean recruit abundance and effectiveness of trapping. The optimization accounts for natural variation by including randomness in overwinter survival (i.e., harsh vs. mild winter; if multiple consecutive years are uploaded) and year-to-year variation in recruit abundance, based on the mean and staandard deviation of recruit abundance in your population across years (if multiple consecutive years are uploaded).

    Why is the objective to minimize abundance at the start of the next trapping season (i.e., after overwinter mortality)?

    This objective function accounts for the estimated strength of size-dependent overwinter mortality in your population. For example, if overwinter mortality affects smaller crabs much more strongly than larger crabs in your population, then it may be less advantageous to remove smaller crabs at the end of the year.

    Preparing your data

    Catch data: catch data should include columns for the date, size of the caught crab (carapace width in mm), type of trap the crab was caught in, and a unique ID associated with the trap the crab was caught in. This unique ID should match trap IDs in the effort data. There should be one row for each caught crab.

    Effort data: effort data should include columns for the date, type of trap, and a unique ID for the trap. There should be one row for each trap set.

    Notes

    • The catch data will be joined to the effort data with the date, trap type, and trap ID. This app will run checks to make sure the catch data match the effort data.
    • This app will use your data to run a model developed by Keller et al. to estimate population dynamics. This model estimated the size selective capture rates of Fukui, minnow, and shrimp traps, so your data should only include catch and effort data that use these trap types. The shrimp traps used in the original model had a 1 cm mesh size.
    • Upload data from one site. The model assumes that crabs are not moving in and out of the site during the trapping period. Crabs only enter the population through recruitment and leave through trapping or natural mortality.
    • You can upload data from one year or multiple years at a site. Years can be continuous (e.g., 2017-2020) or discontinuous (e.g., 2017-2018, 2020). If there are multiple continuous years, the model will estimate size-dependent overwinter mortality.

    Population dynamics at your site are estimated using a model developed by Keller et al. 2026. Below, find more information about the original model, how it is used to estimate parameters based on your data, and key assumptions.

    Size-structured population dynamics model

    The model being fit to your data is based on a model developed by Keller et al. 2026. The model is a hierarchical model that separately models two time series: 1) the latent state, or how the true, unobserved size-structured EGC population changes over time and 2) imperfect observations of the true population (i.e., trap data). An observation process connects these two time series and describes how your trap data are generated. The true population is changing throughout the year (March - November) due to removal, natural mortality, growth in body size, and recruitment, and over the winter (December - February) due to year-specific overwinter mortality and growth in body size. Click the buttons below to learn more about processes in the model.

    Fitting the model to your data

    Rather than re-estimating every parameter, the model fixes growth, natural mortality, and trap selectivity at values from the original study, leaving your data to estimate parameters relevant to your site, including annual adult abundance, annual recruit abundance, recruitment timing, and year-specific overwinter mortality (if multiple years of continuous data have been uploaded). The adult abundance corresponds to the total abundance of adults (ages 1+) in March of each year. The recruit abundance corresponds to the total abundance of recruits for each year, and the recruit timing describes when the recruits enter the model, as the proportion of total recruits entering in April or June.

    Key notes and assumptions

    • The model treats your population as a completely open population, meaning that all recruits enter the population from external sources, rather than through local reproduction.
    • The model accounts for "overdispersion" in traps (i.e., allows for larger variance than expected in trap counts). This feature accounts for aggregation of crabs across space.
    • The model assumes that the rate at which crabs grow in size and die due to natural mortality during the non-winter months is constant across years.
    • This model does not include any density-dependence. Natural mortality during the trapping season and overwinter mortality are a function of crab size but are not density-dependent (i.e., the rate of mortality does not depend on the number of crabs at the site).
    • If multiple continuous years of data are uploaded, size-dependent overwinter mortality links the abundances between years and is often stronger than mortality during the non-winter months. The intensity of overwinter mortality can vary across years.
    • The model assumes recruitment occurs in pulses, with two options of recruits entering the model in April or June. The model estimates the proportion of recruits that enter the population in each of two pulses (e.g., all in April or half in April). The model also assumes that the proportion of recruits entering the population is the same in all years in the data set.
    • The model is derived from Keller et al. 2026 and quantifies uncertainty in model parameters. However, to facilitate faster model estimation within this tool, only some parameter uncertainty is propagated from the original model estimates to the model fit to your data. All model parameters are fixed at the posterior median from Keller et al. 2026, except parameters that scale the overall height of the curves in the size-dependent harvest figure.
